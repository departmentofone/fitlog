FitLog — 10 Visual Design Directions

Same six components mocked up ten different ways: app header, bottom nav, stat/summary card, button set, exercise list item, and goal input + segmented control. Emoji is retired everywhere except where a direction deliberately keeps it in the bottom nav as an intentional choice. Grounded in specific traits from Whoop, Oura, Fitbod, Strong, Apple Fitness, Cronometer and Hevy — synthesized into original systems, not copies.

01
Inspired by Whoop

Blackout

Whoop runs true black with a flat, borderless-feeling card system and lets a single strain/recovery color carry all the meaning — no shadows, no decoration, just huge glanceable numerals over hairline dividers. Applied here as an almost-instrument-panel reading for lifters who want the number, not the chrome: monospace digits, emerald as the only "good" signal, red reserved strictly for destructive action.

02
Inspired by Strong

Ledger

Strong's whole reputation is "does one thing, gets out of the way" — a superclean, function-first Inter layout with almost no chromatic decoration, tight 8px spacing, and tabular numerals so sets line up like a spreadsheet. This direction takes FitLog fully light and utilitarian: one working accent color (a data-terminal orange, not the house emerald) used only for the one thing that matters on screen at a time.

03
Inspired by Fitbod

Recovery Map

Fitbod's signature move is color as information — the muscle-recovery heatmap tells you what's ready to train at a glance. Here that becomes a system-wide habit: every list item carries a small colored status dot (not an emoji) tying it back to a muscle group or readiness state, cards get a soft directional glow instead of a flat fill, and nav icons sit in tinted rounded chips rather than floating loose.

04
Inspired by Apple Fitness

Ring System

Apple Fitness pairs true black with SF Rounded's friendly geometry and expresses everything through colored rings rather than icons or emoji. This direction borrows the rounded-everything warmth and the ring-as-primary-glyph idea — sub-metrics under the headline number get their own tiny arc, and the bottom nav trades tab icons for miniature progress rings in each tab's own color.

05
Inspired by Oura

Field Notes

Oura's real trick isn't the color — it's the restraint: one chromatic accent, hairline borders, and an editorial serif reserved just for the numbers that matter, so they read as considered rather than shouted. To avoid the cliché cream-plus-terracotta combo this look often gets flattened into, Field Notes keeps Oura's paper warmth but pairs it with a cool ink-navy accent and drops decoration from the nav entirely — tab labels alone, underlined when active.

06
Inspired by Cronometer

Clinical

Cronometer leans into being a precision instrument rather than a lifestyle app — dense grids, monospace figures, uppercase micro-labels, muted teal instead of anything celebratory. This direction treats FitLog's data the same way: sharp 6px corners, no shadows, small-caps tracked labels everywhere, and every numeral set in a monospace face so columns of sets and reps actually align.

07
Bold & unconventional — the "go crazy" direction

Signal

None of the reference apps do this — which is the point. This is the direct answer to "go crazy": neo-brutalist construction, thick 3px black borders, hard offset shadows instead of blur, safety-orange as the only color, zero corner radius. It reframes destructive actions and PR moments as unmissable rather than polite, which suits the adrenaline of a training session far more than a soft rounded card ever could.

08
Swiss grid × athletic scoreboard

Trackside

Borrows the International Typographic Style discipline that underlies good sports-timing displays — bold condensed grotesque set in all caps, hairline black rule lines instead of cards-on-cards, a single race-red accent. The bottom nav swaps icons for numbered tabs (01/02/03), like a heat sheet, which reads as confident and sport-specific rather than generic-app.

09
Glassmorphism, tuned to fitness

Aurora Glass

Takes the layered, floating-depth feeling several premium wellness apps use for their hero surfaces — frosted translucent cards over soft ambient glow — but deliberately swaps the usual purple-to-blue gradient for an emerald/amber duotone so it still reads as "training," not "meditation app." Real elevation comes from backdrop blur and a top highlight border rather than a drop shadow.

10
Gamified & energetic — NTC-style

Podium

Where most of the other directions restrain color to one accent, Podium goes the other way on purpose: a multi-color system (green for effort, gold for streaks/achievements, coral for danger) across big chunky rounded shapes and a bold rounded display face, closer to a training-club app that wants logging a set to feel like a small win. Kept emoji in the nav deliberately here — bold color chips make them feel chosen, not left over.
